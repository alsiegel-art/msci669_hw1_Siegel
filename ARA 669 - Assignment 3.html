Read & reflect

Read the article “Do native land crabs hinder forest regeneration on Socorro Island?” (Luna-Zuniga et al. 2025). Describe: [2 pt]

Forests need recruitment. They’re potentially seing low regen. Is it because of land crabs eating seeds? DBH distribution, says that most species have more adults than seedlings. They did some exclusions, crabs mainly consume two species. Big % of those seeds. Germination is super low. Crabs don’t seem to be the only or even main thing - it’s low germination rate.

The aim of the study was to determine whether there is a lack of tree recruitment and how this varies among forest types on the island, and test whether this crab is the main recruitment barrier for common tree species.

Did they actually test that? It sounds like no for the different forest types for most of what they covered in the abstract, but I need to keep reading. 3 types of forest.

They evaluated all 3 forest types. 5 species. A total of 18 vegetation plots 50x10m. Counted individuals in 3 life stages for all species.

  1. The primary question(s) the authors are trying to answer. The authors wanted to know if 3 forest types on this island were recruitment limited, if that differed by forest type, and if land crabs were the main recruitment barrier.

  2. The experimental and sampling design. They had one site - the island. 3 forest types. 18 total plots - 6 per site. And they counted and grouped 5 species by life stage in all plots. But, of those 18 plots, they had 16 in 2023 and 2 in 2024 - introducing time. They also compared to an earlier study, but I’m ignoring that from here out because it was not key to addressing their questions. That was all for the recruitment question. For the crab question, they have an experiment for each of their 5 species. There are 2 possible treatments - control and crab exclosure. They have 6 sites for Ilex, 4 for Guettarda and Sideroxylon, and 3 for Psidium. They spread sites and distributed over an elevation range. Each of these sites has 10 pairs of exclosures/controls, which I’ll call plots. Each plot had 10 seeds in it. In this experiment, they also monitored seed loss over a short timespan, and then recruitment (survival of emerging seedlings) over a longer timespan, multiple times (after each of 3 seed placements). These seed placements differed in season - so time comes in again.

  3. The statistical approach used. For their recruitment survey, they used GLMM (generalized)’s, with a negative binomial distribution on counts ~ life stage (categorical, 3 levels) + species (categorical, 5 levels) + forest type (categorical, 3 levels) with interactions between species and both other predictor variables. + a random effect of ID. For DBH distributions they used curve-fitting.

For the crab experiment (and the survival experiment afterwards in the same plots), they used GLMM (generalized) with a binomial distribution on proportion of seeds remaining (proportion, from 0-1 or 0-100/initial # seeds, getting odds of survival for an individual seed) ~ treatment (true/false) * species (categorical, 4 levels). + a random factor, treatment ID (0,1). They don’t explicitly say that treatment and species were included separately, but I’m assuming they were.

They counted so few seedlings they couldn’t do any real stats on emergence and survival.

They also compared the number of seedlings and the remaining seeds after the experiment by treatment using chisquare tests, predictive variables being treatment, species, and an interaction.

Imagine you are reviewing this manuscript prior to its publication. Provide a constructive critique of the statistical analyses as they relate to the experimental and sampling design. What did they do well? What would be improved? [3 pt]

These statistics are thoughtful in some ways, particularly in how they consider the questions they’re trying to ask and the ecology of the area. They only included variables that were key to the experimental design or have a clear ecological reason to be included. But, some statistics couldn’t be run due to low counts, and they should have done more testing and confirmation that these were the best possible versions of each model, or at least reported such in the supplement.

For the recruitment survey, assuming the responses are linear, a GLMM makes sense if residuals aren’t evenly distributed and there are random effects. Negative binomial for counts makes sense, especially because they could get zeros. Including forest type as a predictor variable makes sense - one of their main questions was if things differ between forest types, so it wouldn’t have worked as a random effect. Including species type as a fixed instead of random effect might make sense and might not. It wasn’t one of their key questions, but knowing how species differ may be important - although it could have been a random effect because it has more than 4 levels (5). Life stage has only 3 levels, and is clearly important, so it had to be a fixed effect instead of random. Interactions between species and the other variables and the random effect of ID are where I’m less confident. ID as a random effect might be overkill if they laid out their plots independently and well - if they checked the model and it was useful, it’s fine to stay, but I would check. For the interactions, their main question was how forest types differ, not how species differ, so I would have swapped out the interaction between species and life stage for one between forest type and life stage if I kept it at all. But it may be that they didn’t accurately explain their goals in the introduction and species type is critical, in which case it might be okay.

For the crab experiment, GLMM and a binomial distribution for proportion data makes sense. Treatment as a fixed effect makes sense - including species here makes sense ecologically, and because they didn’t necessarily set up these plots across different forest types that wasn’t included. They also only had 4 species here, so including it as a random effect wasn’t a great option anyways. Again ID as a random effect might not be needed, but is worth including at least in an early version of the model to test.

One major complaint I have is that their last experiment, on seedling emergence and survival after the exclusion experiment concluded, they have no statistics. They clearly either didn’t expect such low germination, or didn’t think far enough ahead to have enough replication to deal with that. If it’s the latter, that’s a problem. If it’s the former, there’s not much they could have done about it. But that’s sampling design anyways, not how they ran the statistics.

Model development

Based on the experimental and sampling design, which variables should be considered as fixed effects? Which could be considered as random effects? What distributions should be considered? [1 pt]

For this analysis, the response variable being either seeds remaining or seeds removed, or proportion of seeds remaining/removed. To me what makes the most sense is proportion of seeds removed - for percentages we want either beta or binomial. Here, it’s binomial, because it’s a count of successes vs failures.

Species should be fixed, because there are only 4, and we might be interested to know how the crabs impact different species differently. Site might not be needed at all, if it is included it should be a random effect because we don’t need to know each sites specific impacts or really care what the differences between sites are. ID_treatment, if included, would also be a random effect. It’s almost like plot, with two measurements per plot (each treatment). If it was included, it should probably be nested within site. Treatment is a fixed effect. Lastly altitude, probably doesn’t need to be included. If it is included, it has to be as a fixed effect because it’s numeric. It might be worth including if it’s expected ecologically to matter in this system, just as a check, but probably isn’t needed.

So I suggest: Binomial, response (proportion of seeds removed) ~ Species + Treatment + (1|Site/ID_Treatment) The random effect could also be (Site/ID_Treatment|1), it may be worth looking at plots of some relationships to see which seems more useful.
